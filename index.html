Why Supplement Brands Rarely Disclose Their Contract Manufacturers

Almost every supplement brand hires another company to make its products, and almost none of them will tell you which company that is. The manufacturer is named nowhere on the label, nowhere on the website, and nowhere in the marketing. The brand will talk at length about its ingredients, its testing and its values, and stay silent about the factory that actually made the product. That silence is worth examining.

The first reason brands stay silent is competition. A manufacturer is a business relationship, and a brand that names its manufacturer is telling its competitors where to go for the same product. The fear is that a rival will approach the same factory, order the same formula, and undercut the brand on price. That fear is not unreasonable, but it is a business concern, not a buyer concern, and it is paid for by the buyer in the form of information they cannot check.

The second reason is liability. A brand that names its manufacturer makes it easier for a buyer to trace a problem to its source, and easier for a regulator to do the same. A brand that keeps the manufacturer anonymous keeps a layer of distance between itself and the factory floor. If a batch is contaminated or a dose is wrong, the brand can describe the problem as a supplier issue and move on, without the buyer ever learning which supplier was involved. The anonymity protects the brand. It does not protect the buyer.

The third reason is that the manufacturer may not want to be named either. Some contract manufacturers serve dozens of brands, including brands that compete with each other, and they prefer not to be publicly linked to any single one. Others have had quality problems in the past and would rather keep a low profile. The brand and the manufacturer may both prefer the arrangement to stay private, and the buyer is the only party in the transaction who loses anything from that privacy.

The fourth reason is the simplest: the law does not require disclosure. A supplement label must name the distributor, but it does not have to name the manufacturer. Brands disclose what the law requires and keep the rest private, because there is no penalty for silence and no reward for openness. The result is an industry where the most important fact about a product — who actually made it — is the fact buyers are least likely to learn.

There are ways to find out anyway. A certificate of analysis sometimes names the laboratory that tested the product, and a laboratory's client list can narrow the field. A brand's own marketing may mention a facility, a region or a certification that points to a specific manufacturer. A direct question to the brand, asking who manufactures the product and where, is a reasonable request, and the quality of the answer is itself informative. A brand that answers promptly and precisely is one that has nothing to hide. A brand that refuses, or that responds with a generic statement about quality, is telling you something about how it treats its buyers.

The manufacturer is not a trade secret. It is the company that made the thing you are about to swallow, and knowing who it is lets you check its record, its certifications and its history. A brand that hides that information is not protecting a recipe. It is protecting itself from scrutiny, and the buyer who asks the question anyway is the buyer who cannot be kept in the dark.
